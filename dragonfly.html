Dragonfly: Building and Scaling a Service Business

I helped grow and operate a family-owned pest and mosquito control company. As the business expanded, the work became more complex: people, routes, equipment, customers, training and management decisions all had to fit together.

The business experience is real. Financial figures below illustrate an analytical scenario built retrospectively, not Dragonfly's historical sales or profit.

Executive overview

From founder-dependent operations to a more scalable service business.

Growth changed the management problem. A small crew could coordinate through constant personal attention. A larger operation required reliable staffing, clearer expectations, service readiness, customer follow-through and managers who could work from shared priorities.

Workforce

I recruited, interviewed, hired, onboarded, coached and handled performance issues. As headcount expanded, delegation and clear management expectations became essential.

Daily execution

I helped coordinate customer intake and estimates, dispatch, schedules, daily rerouting, escalations and technician readiness. A schedule was useful only if the people and equipment were available to deliver it.

Operating backbone

I worked across procedures, records, purchasing, vendors, inventory, payroll coordination and software choices. The objective was to make repeatable work less dependent on anyone's memory.

Three operational decisions

What business growth required.

01 / PEOPLE

Workforce scaling and frontline leadership

Situation: Adding employees increased service capacity but also multiplied training, scheduling and accountability needs.

My contribution: I worked on hiring, onboarding, coaching, employee readiness and performance management, alongside other family operators. Different employees needed different support: training for skill gaps, clearer expectations for execution gaps and accountability when repeated issues persisted.

Trade-off: Hiring without readiness and supervision creates avoidable service failures. I treated role clarity and practical coaching as part of the capacity decision.

Observed scope: The business grew to approximately 21 employees, excluding the two operators. Specific cost savings or quality improvement rates have not been audited.

02 / ROUTES

Service capacity, scheduling and dispatch

Situation: Service requests competed for the same technicians, vehicles, equipment and time. Daily disruptions could affect multiple customers.

My contribution: I handled scheduling, dispatch coordination, rerouting, customer communication and service exceptions, weighing what could actually be completed rather than relying on a perfect-looking route plan.

Trade-off: Additional driving can absorb the capacity created by another hire. Reliable routing depends on location, service duration and practical buffers, not job count alone.

Observed scope: These responsibilities formed part of day-to-day operations. The numerical travel-time scenarios below were developed later to examine the trade-off.

03 / SYSTEMS

Business infrastructure that could support growth

Situation: As more people shared customer, inventory, route and service information, informal processes became harder to sustain.

My contribution: I helped improve operating routines, team expectations, records, coordination and software-supported workflows. My exposure included FieldRoutes, GorillaDesk, PestPac, Jobber, Route4Me, QuickBooks Online, RouteOp, Samsara and Verizon Connect, used or evaluated in different contexts.

Trade-off: Buying software does not solve unclear ownership or inconsistent work practices. Process design and adoption had to accompany tooling.

Observed scope: Operational process participation and software exposure, not a claim of implementing every named platform.

Decision framework

Evaluate the trade-offs before expanding.

Hire or optimize?

Compare incremental loaded payroll and fleet costs with jobs completed and utilization. Capacity that exists on paper may not survive service windows, customer cancellations or uneven travel.

New territory or route density?

Test customer concentration, drive time, dispatch support and available technician hours. An adjacent service cluster can be more attractive than scattered new jobs.

Raise prices or shift mix?

Model realized ticket size against lost demand, retention, service duration and material usage. Higher ticket value is not automatically more profit.

Under the illustrated assumptions, redesigning existing routes deserves testing before hiring or opening a distant territory. That conclusion would change if new demand is weak, promised customer windows cannot be met, employee travel cannot be reduced or service quality deteriorates.

Additional leadership evidence

Different industries, consistent operating discipline.

Safe Haven Security Services

As Regional Sales Manager, I led roughly 15 team members across five markets. My focus included recruiting, onboarding, coaching, scheduling, KPI reviews and the critical handoff between sales representatives, technicians and completed installations. Lead volume alone did not establish installation quality or customer outcomes.

Systems: SecurityTrax, Alarm.com and Excel reporting.

TogetherHealth

I earned President's Club recognition and ranked first in individual sales among approximately 350 representatives, then supervised about 25 licensed professionals. During the Annual Enrollment Period, my team ranked first. My leadership included coaching, performance management, scheduling, call quality and compliance-conscious sales practices.

The leadership contribution is performance and quality management, not just a sales ranking.

Supporting analysis

Methods, evidence and limitations.

Historical statements reflect firsthand operational experience. The financial analysis is a new scenario workbook, not a transcription of accounting, customer, payroll or CRM data. All revenue, costs, utilization, retention and profitability figures are modeled rather than audited company outcomes. The workbook includes source notes, input controls and scenario reconciliations.

The point of the work is how I think through growth: identify constraints, compare alternatives, understand unit economics and put practical management systems behind a decision.
