Service Manager Operating System

I advised a field-service manager on how to organize priorities, commitments and team communication—then designed and configured Google Workspace and Gemini workflows to help keep important work visible throughout the day.

Operational problem

A field-service manager balances customer escalations, vendor responses, scheduling requests, technicians and leadership messages while spending substantial time away from email. Simple unread or starred flags do not communicate who owns the next action, whether a reply actually resolves a problem, or what should be surfaced urgently.

My role: workflow consultant, solution designer and configurator. I worked from the manager's real responsibilities—vendors, key accounts, leadership, technicians, scheduling, customer escalations and urgent service issues. I mapped what needed immediate attention, what he personally owed, what he was waiting on and what needed a follow-up. Then I translated that operating model into Gmail, Gemini and Google Workspace Studio rules, labels, alerts and manager briefings.

The business rule behind the system

An outgoing reply is not proof that a task is completed or transferred. The classifier must check the full thread, current responsibility and remaining action before changing the status.

My goal was to keep work visible until the responsible person had truly finished it—not to make an inbox look tidy.

Solution

Make priorities, promises and unfinished work visible every day.

Classify

State + identity

I designed six exclusive work states—ACTION, WAITING, FOLLOW_UP, INFO, RESOLVED and NEEDS_REVIEW—while keeping customer, vendor and other source labels intact. Urgent work is flagged separately so nothing critical gets buried.

Alert

Urgent operations

Separate key customer and national-account issues, time-sensitive service problems, vendor decisions and leadership escalations from ordinary updates. The workflow was designed to surface these exceptions through Gmail and Google Chat.

Follow through

Open-loop discipline

Track commitments until they are resolved or explicitly handed off. I designed a 6:40 AM morning brief, 12:15 PM field checkpoint and 4:30 PM open-loops review, with priority, owner and next action at the center.

Consulting & solution design

I designed the management process before automating the inbox.

Operating priorities & team accountability

I structured the manager's information flow by business importance: key accounts and urgent customer risks, vendor and management decisions, technician needs and scheduling requests. I developed ownership rules so tasks were assigned to the right person instead of disappearing into read messages.

Daily decision support

The operating rhythm was designed to answer three questions: What needs my attention now? What did I promise that is still open? What am I waiting on from somebody else? Briefings and label rules were designed around those decisions—not just email volume.

Exception handling & safeguards

I addressed duplicate labels, legacy states, inbox-bypass behavior, Studio step limits and the risk of classifying a thread from its newest reply alone. Unclear work routes to human review rather than an invented completion state.

Iterative configuration

I designed and configured workflow components, tested edge cases with the manager's operating priorities in mind and refined prompts and rules where behavior was inconsistent. Implementation and reliability were still being evaluated during the documented work.

Illustrative ownership check (fictional example)

Example input: "I will inspect the location tomorrow, then decide whether to approve the vendor request." This is a fictional test message illustrating the rule, not a retrieved customer email or a documented model output.

Required behavior: Keep the manager in ACTION, because the inspection remains his task. Do not switch to WAITING solely because his email was the newest message. If the owner cannot be established, select NEEDS_REVIEW. This is the specified acceptance rule, not evidence of a passed automated test.

What was delivered and how I evaluated it

This was real operational consulting and hands-on configuration, including an ownership model, classification logic, urgent routing, manager briefing workflows and troubleshooting. I refined the rules against observed edge cases and documented criteria for further testing. End-to-end production reliability, classification accuracy and time savings were not independently measured.

Next phase · Performance evaluation

From configured workflow to measured operating performance.

I have developed a controlled evaluation design for the Service Manager workflow. The plan defines how to check current-state accuracy, urgent issue recognition, wrongly cleared commitments, overdue follow-up visibility, manager review time and human correction effort. It also includes 30 synthetic acceptance scenarios, a measurement workbook, a release checklist and a rollback approach.

Measurement plan

Measure obligations, not inbox activity.

The baseline and any pilot must use the same case definitions, a documented sample and a human-reviewed reference state. A reply does not automatically mean that work is complete.

Implementation controls

Test exceptions before wider use.

The proposed pilot retains manager review for uncertain ownership, urgent issues and workflow failures. Actual test outcomes, operating costs and adoption would be recorded before evaluating business value.

Evaluation design is prepared. A new live pilot, measured results and public reporting of employer-specific outcomes still require organizational authorization and operating observations. No time-savings or production accuracy result is claimed.
